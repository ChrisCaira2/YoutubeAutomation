# Channel Strategy: The Athletics Archive (Phase 0, approved)

As of 2026-10-26. Approved by the channel owner on 2026-10-26.

## Niche

**The biggest moments in sports history — great games, record-breaking performances and impossible upsets — rebuilt play by play and explained with the numbers.**
Multi-sport, US-focused (baseball, American football, basketball, hockey, boxing, golf, tennis, the Olympics), with the occasional global moment. Analytics decide later which sports get more episodes.

## The angle that makes us different

"Greatest moments" is a crowded, countdown-heavy space, and countdown lists are exactly the template-style content YouTube demonetizes. We do the opposite: **one moment per video, reconstructed in depth**.
- **Rebuild it:** animated diagrams of the play, the scoreboard ticking, the clock running down.
- **Measure it:** how unlikely it really was (win probability, odds, how long the record stood, the math behind it).
- **Explain it:** what led up to it, and what changed in the sport afterward.
- **Host it:** an original illustrated professor who "replays the tape" for you.

## Audience

Adults 25–55, mostly US, sports fans who enjoy nostalgia, debate and "I didn't know that" detail. Long-form on TV and laptop, Shorts on phone.

## Promise

"You've seen the highlight. Here's what actually happened — and why it almost didn't."

## Editorial voice

- Warm, dramatic, precise. A favorite professor narrating a thriller, not a hype channel.
- Every number sourced (box scores, official records, contemporary newspapers).
- Myth-busts openly ("Everyone remembers X. The box score says Y.").
- Respectful to athletes; no mockery, no outrage bait, no gambling promotion.

## Recurring series

| Series | Format | Share of long-form |
|---|---|---|
| **Instant Replay** | One great game, rebuilt from the setup to the final play | ~40% |
| **Record Breakers** | How a record was set, how unlikely it was, and whether it can fall | ~35% |
| **Miracles & Upsets** | The underdog win: the odds, the turning point, the aftermath | ~25% |

Shorts: **"One Moment"** — a single play or number in 20–45 seconds, standalone, looping back to its first frame; some tease the matching long-form episode.

## How we show real moments without footage (the key constraint)

Broadcast footage and famous radio/TV calls are owned by leagues and networks and get claimed by Content ID, so we never use them. AI-generated likenesses of real athletes are also out (the brief's own rule, and realistic ones would need YouTube's AI disclosure). What we use instead:

| Visual | Use | Rights |
|---|---|---|
| Animated play diagrams, court/field maps, scoreboards, clocks | The core of every reconstruction | Ours |
| Stat cards, win-probability and record-progression charts | The "how unlikely" analysis | Ours |
| Illustrated scenes with **faceless, generic** figures (silhouettes, no real faces, no logos) | Mood and action between diagrams | Ours, clearly illustrated, so no disclosure needed |
| Real photos from `assets/photos/manifest.json` | Where the narrator is talking about the thing shown | Recorded source and license per photo, credited on screen and in the description |
| Public-domain archive photos (US, published 1930 or earlier) | Moments before 1931 (e.g., Babe Ruth's 1927 season) | Public domain; check each photo's rights statement |
| Creative Commons photos from Wikimedia Commons | Showing the real athlete or venue where one exists | Attribution in the description; check each license |
| The professor | Opens, chapter resets, the big reveal, the close | Ours |

Thumbnails: the professor, a big number or scoreline, and a faceless action silhouette. No athlete photos in thumbnails.

Note: I'm not a lawyer. Using a real athlete's name and a properly licensed photo to tell a factual story is standard documentary practice, but if the channel grows, a short consult on publicity rights is worth it.

## Cadence

Two long-form videos (8–12 min) and one or two Shorts per week, scaled only after the quality gates and your approvals run smoothly.

## Policy guardrails (checked 2026-10-26)

- YouTube's "inauthentic content" rule (renamed July 15, 2025; clarified July 16, 2026) demonetizes channels with too much generic, template-based or distressing content, including "image slideshows or scrolling text with little value" ([YouTube help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392), [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/20/youtube-clarifies-policies-around-ai-slop-and-upsetting-videos/)). Our defense: one deep story per video, sourced analysis, frequent cuts between varied layouts, a recurring character, rotating formats, no countdown lists. Still frames with no zooms are the owner's choice (2026-11-02), so the analysis has to carry the "value" test.
- The July 2026 rule on "AI personas" targets AI representations of real people on finance, legal or health topics. Our professor is fictional and covers sports, so it does not apply.
- AI disclosure is needed for realistic AI depictions of real people or events ([YouTube help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491)). Keeping all generated art clearly illustrated and faceless avoids that.
